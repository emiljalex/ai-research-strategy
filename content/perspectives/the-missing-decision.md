A team can agree on every word in an interview transcript and still disagree about what to build. Imagine a reader who says, “I found the entrance. I still left because I could only come after six.” One person hears a story about finding the building. Another hears a story about opening hours. Those readings would send a library in quite different directions.

## The choice that disappears

Suppose the account travels through a sequence of tools. One extracts a passage, another assigns a label, and a third groups the labels into a recommendation. The final paragraph might say, “Name the entrance in the pickup notice.” It sounds like a modest copy edit. Inside that sentence sits a much larger decision about which account counts as evidence for which problem.

A citation can take the team back to the original words. The harder question is whether anyone can change the label and see what else would have to change. If the recommendation stays fixed, the citation serves a reader while the decision remains out of reach.

## A question we can put to work

Pick one recommendation from a research readout. Identify the smallest change in interpretation that could make the team reconsider it. Who has the evidence to notice that change? Who can make it? Which decisions depend on it?

[Run the library example in the Lab](/lab/#workbench). Its three invented accounts make the mechanism small enough to inspect. The broader question remains open. Which decisions deserve this level of visibility when there are hundreds of accounts, several languages, and people who disagree in good faith?
