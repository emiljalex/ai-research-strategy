Choose a decision someone might implement this week. Work backward until you find a moment where one person or tool handed an interpretation to another. Complete the five entries below. A short answer in ordinary language is enough.

## 1. Name the receiving decision

Write down who will act and what they could do differently. “The library editor will decide whether to change the pickup notice” is specific enough. “Improve the pickup experience” leaves several decisions bundled together.

## 2. Preserve the source and its context

Record the smallest passage that can support or contradict the handoff. Include the question that prompted it or the condition under which it applies. In the library example, “I found the entrance” and “I could only come after six” belong together. Keeping just one clause changes the available interpretations.

## 3. Write the interpretation in its own field

Name the operation that turned the source into a claim. In this example it is assigning a single label to an account. Write the chosen label and one plausible alternative. This makes a disagreement inspectable before it becomes a dispute about the whole report.

## 4. Assign the right to intervene

Name someone who can change that interpretation and state what they must preserve. A reviewer can move an account from “Finding the entrance” to “Opening hours” if the original account and prior label remain visible. If the evidence cannot settle a disagreement, record the alternatives for judgment.

## 5. Spell out the consequence

Finish this sentence. “If this interpretation changes, we must revisit ___ before ___.” For the pickup notice, recompute the summary and revisit the proposed copy change before the editor acts.

**Try the worksheet.** [Change one label in the Lab](/lab/#workbench), then read the revision history. Could a colleague reconstruct what you changed, what stayed fixed, and why the next decision changed?
