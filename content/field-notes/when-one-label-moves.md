This is a bench note about the [Intervention Lab](/lab/#workbench). The accounts are invented. The labels and decision rule are fixed in the example, so anyone can reproduce the result.

## Set up the comparison

Start from the original state. Two accounts carry the label “Finding the entrance.” One carries “Hold expiration.” The example proposes testing an entrance cue because its rule selects a response only when one label has a unique lead. That rule is deliberately simple enough to inspect.

## Make one edit

Inspect Account B. The speaker says they found the entrance and could only come after six. Change the label from “Finding the entrance” to “Opening hours” and apply the revision. Leave the other accounts alone.

## Observe the result

The original source text stays in place. Each of the three explanations now has one account. The recommendation changes from testing an entrance cue to collecting another account before choosing a change. The revision history identifies the account, the previous label, and its replacement. Undoing the revision restores the original recommendation.

## Where the example breaks down

A real account can support several interpretations at once. A change that serves a single reader might matter more than one that serves a larger group. Counting labels answers neither question. A reviewer can correct a label inside this model, but cannot correct the model's assumption that each account belongs in exactly one category.

A next experiment would let a reviewer retain two interpretations of the same passage. The interesting observation would be whether a downstream decision can preserve that disagreement all the way to the person who must act.
